\title{How Shared Outputs Shape Source-Specific Evidence in In-Context Learning}
\author{Anonymous research draft}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}

An in-context learner can receive demonstrations from sources that use the same labels for different rules. How does it form the evidence needed to answer for one source? We find that the output vocabulary shapes learned relations during demonstration processing. Earlier answers can transmit one source's input–label associations into another source's evidence, giving that evidence a history that affects prediction. Causal interventions predict output-naming effects at the tested evidence interfaces and establish the historical pathway's contribution to ordinary judgments. This account also predicts different effects of source-separation policies. In shared-output tasks, selectively limiting answer-level sharing improves mean rule-use accuracy, while broader isolation can impair joint use. Public source–output information also affects separately formed rules before query reading, and equivalent formation backgrounds produce different joint usability. Controlled and real-text experiments across three model families, including independent 32B confirmation, connect these formation processes to source-conditioned behavior. The results explain how learned relations acquire a source provenance and how their formation constrains subsequent evidence selection.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

A review can praise a restaurant's food while criticizing its service. Two annotators may use the same binary labels but apply different judgment rules to that review. An in-context learner receives both annotators' examples and is asked to predict for one of them. It must infer the relevant input--output relation and use it on a new comment. Similar questions arise whenever demonstrations encode several tasks, conventions, or perspectives in one context. Understanding which evidence governs the answer requires understanding how those demonstrated relations become available to the model.

\citet{cho2025revisitingincontextlearninginference} traced how input information enters label states and is retrieved by a query. \citet{bakalova2025contextualizethenaggregatecircuitsincontextlearning} showed that cross-demonstration contextualization shapes task information before aggregation. In a multi-source context, these processes create a distinction between a demonstration's positional owner and the history of relations represented there. Source A's tokens can contain information influenced by B's examples. The fact that a query reads A's positions leaves open how B's supervision shaped what it reads.

This distinction motivates our question: \textbf{how do shared output identities affect the formation and use of source-specific evidence?} Output words participate in the representations formed during demonstration processing. When different sources share them, changing one source's rule may change the evidence available at another source's positions. At the same time, surrounding context can supply information that helps several rules work together. An account of source-conditioned ICL should explain both the relations entering that evidence and the effects of sharing or separating them.

We study this process by changing one source's demonstrated relation while holding the other source and the query fixed. Whole-query access restrictions and message interchanges distinguish positional access from causal dependence on a source's rule. A historical answer-to-label pathway predicts output-namespace counterfactuals at these interfaces in new contexts with a fixed coefficient. Removing the corresponding operation during ordinary processing then tests whether it contributes to native predictions. These experiments establish output-dependent evidence formation, the first part of our account.

We next keep output words, positions, and input multisets fixed while changing input--label associations. These counterfactuals identify relational content carried through the pathway. The effects persist under full-source reading and on real sentiment text. The formation account then yields a functional prediction: selectively limiting other-source answer formation, isolating complete source histories, and restricting query access should have different consequences. We test these policies for both requested sources and retain their gains and tradeoffs.

Finally, we examine what can support joint rule use when private supervision forms independently. Public source--output relations can help through formation, and information-equivalent public backgrounds change how well independently formed source evidence works together. The same formation, native-operation, and real-text function tests are independently confirmed in Qwen3-32B. Together, the experiments connect three findings: output identities affect historical formation, the historical messages carry learned associations, and formation and reading impose different functional constraints. This account explains source-conditioned computation through the provenance and use of learned relations, including when the model answers correctly.

\section{Measuring which source's relations influence a prediction}\label{measuring-which-sources-relations-influence-a-prediction}

\subsection{Sources, rules, and materials}\label{sources-rules-and-materials}

The current sentiment and dual-aspect experiments contain eight demonstrations from each source and eight novel queries. In shared-word conditions both sources use \texttt{no/\allowbreak{}yes}, with private label polarity balanced across contexts; separate-word conditions keep A's words and give B \texttt{pass/\allowbreak{}flag}; earlier Measuring Hate Speech experiments use \texttt{safe/\allowbreak{}toxic}. A's raw examples and labels are held fixed; paired counterfactuals change B. A single-aspect sentiment task separates association from output naming. A dual-aspect task independently varies food and service sentiment, B's inferred criterion, and its private output convention. Original SST5 reviews supply a natural-text test with independently sampled demonstrations and official held-out queries.

All models are frozen: Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-32B post-trained hybrid checkpoints, Mistral-7B-v0.3 base, and Llama-3.1-8B base. We score the serialized task as a continuation using FP32 eager attention. Confirmation uses new materials after discovery, without selecting models, contexts, or seeds by correctness. Model revisions, actual token streams, and complete split rules are recorded in the \href{PAPER_APPENDIX.md}{appendix}.

\subsection{Positional access and functional provenance}\label{positional-access-and-functional-provenance}

For a fixed common prompt background, let \(D_A\) and \(D_B\) denote the demonstrations owned by sources A and B. \(H_A\) and \(H_B\) denote their contextualized key/value states, grouped by positional ownership. We call these available demonstration states evidence. Formation is their computation during prefix processing, and reading is their subsequent use by query tokens. Let \(q\) denote the requested source and new input, and \(z\) the output-label logit difference. We write

\[
\begin{aligned}H_A&=F_A(D_A,D_B),\quad H_B=F_B(D_A,D_B),\\ z&=R(q,H_A,H_B).\end{aligned}
\]

\(F_A\) and \(F_B\) denote prefix computations at the two source groups, and \(R\) denotes query computation under the specified access graph. For A's no/yes outputs, \(z\) is yes minus no. Relational provenance is measured as causal dependence on a source's demonstrated input--label association. We change B's pairing while fixing A and the query, then prevent every query position from accessing B positions. A remaining B-rule effect reaches the prediction through the available contextualized graph.

Under this restriction, changing B's rule changes 17.6\% {[}12.1, 23.8{]} of A's answers in 32 independent Qwen3-8B Measuring Hate Speech contexts (E90). Separating B's output vocabulary reduces the inherited effect even though A's raw demonstrations and labels are unchanged. Source identity also has native causal effects in earlier interventions. Thus the result concerns the provenance of contextualized evidence, rather than a model completely ignoring source names.

\subsection{Evaluation and reproducibility}\label{evaluation-and-reproducibility}

Intervals use 10,000 context-bootstrap samples, with contexts as the sampling units. We retain every predefined condition and check consumed tokens and masks, cached/full-token scores, self interventions, and graph zeros. Independent analyses reconstruct raw quantities and gold/access layouts before reading summaries. Appendix A specifies serialization, analysis, and reproducibility details.

\section{How output identities shape historical evidence}\label{how-output-identities-shape-historical-evidence}

\subsection{A frozen formation-path prediction}\label{a-frozen-formation-path-prediction}

We compare shared-output and separate-output prefixes, keeping A's examples and output words identical. Counterfactual donor states replace historical B-answer-region K, V, or KV \textbf{only in messages consumed at A's label positions} during prefix processing. Subsequent states evolve normally. Interchanging the resulting A-label caches into one otherwise pristine recipient tests the specified formation interface.

The prediction has coefficient one: the message-intervention effect predicts the actual namespace counterfactual at that interface. No head, layer, strength, or regression coefficient is selected. We report raw effects and rule double contrasts, which subtract the namespace effect between two B-association worlds. This separates relation-dependent naming effects from a common output shift.

In new contexts, K reconstruction gives rule-double-contrast correlations .969 and .966 for Qwen3-8B and Mistral on the controlled Measuring Hate Speech task; KV gives .985 and .977. The same frozen mechanism remains predictive on controlled sentiment and real SST5 text. These are actual-donor causal reconstructions: their target is a specified interface effect, and residuals are measured against the coefficient-one identity prediction.

\subsection{Testing the native operation}\label{testing-the-native-operation}

Reconstruction alone does not establish native contribution. We therefore block historical B-answer access at A-label receivers during ordinary prefix processing, using the same fixed mask at every layer. There is no donor activation. A complementary test blocks all B-to-A prefix access, then reopens only the historical answer-to-label path. Within-A answer removal and single-source private functions control for disturbance to general historical learning.

\section{Historical messages carry learned associations}\label{historical-messages-carry-learned-associations}

\subsection{Fixed-word relational counterfactuals}\label{fixed-word-relational-counterfactuals}

Namespace changes might alter output-related signals without changing learned relational content. We therefore hold B's label stream, source names, positions, and input multiset fixed, and reassign comments across opposite sentiment classes. A matched within-class reassignment moves the same number of inputs without reversing the association. A's raw evidence and novel queries stay fixed; paired token geometry is matched before model scoring.

For each world, we measure the correct-label-oriented query effect, then compare its magnitude between cross-class and within-class reassignment. Historical answer KV has an excess effect of .618 {[}.446, .813{]} nats in Qwen and .404 {[}.313, .499{]} in Mistral under A-only reading. V carries substantial content, while the namespace reconstruction is more accurately reproduced through K/KV. This dissociates the intervention's relation content from its output-identity effect without assuming independent internal components.

The relation effect also holds when queries can read both sources. Moreover, crossing A-position caches formed under shared/separate namespaces with B-position caches from the same backgrounds changes native B-rule dependence. The signed interaction is positive in the three smaller checkpoints and negative in Qwen3-14B (−.686 {[}−1.133, −.237{]} nats). The model therefore consumes jointly formed evidence; its source caches are not independent stores whose effects can be assumed additive.

The answer-region pathway reproduces part of the relation effect. Full label interchanges have additional effects, and the signed relation contrasts vary by model. These distinctions motivate measuring complete functional behavior in the next experiment, rather than equating a message effect with a fully transferred rule.

\subsection{Reliable private conventions on natural text}\label{reliable-private-conventions-on-natural-text}

We repeat the frozen 43-condition engine on original extreme-polarity SST5 reviews. Demonstration pools are disjoint text-hash partitions of training; discovery and confirmation queries use official validation and test splits. Gold sentiment and token geometry determine construction, without screening model outputs. Positive and inverted private conventions have single-source accuracies of 88.3--99.2\% across the four checkpoints (Appendix D).

The same coefficient-one KV formation reconstruction gives rule correlations .931/.948/.930/.966 in Qwen3-8B, Mistral, Llama, and Qwen3-14B, respectively. Fixed-output association excess magnitudes are positive in every family. Llama's signed relation contrast is negative (−.109 {[}−.194, −.024{]} nats): transmitted content is not uniformly a correction of the requested rule. SST5 provides a functionally usable natural-text replication; these constructed conventions do not recover individual human preferences.

\section{How formation constrains source-conditioned rule use}\label{how-formation-constrains-source-conditioned-rule-use}

\subsection{Competing formation and reading policies}\label{competing-formation-and-reading-policies}

The formation account predicts different consequences for three operations: selectively limiting other sources' answer messages, removing all cross-source formation, and restricting only query access. The first tests whether limiting answer sharing protects private mappings while retaining other contextual paths; the second can also remove useful shared information; the third acts on evidence already formed.

We fix three prefix policies---native, symmetric other-source answer removal at label receivers, and all cross-source removal---and two whole-query scopes. Both source requests use the same prefix. On dual-aspect comments, B's food/service criterion and same/opposite private convention vary independently. The primary averages two-candidate accuracy over both sources, worlds, and queries within each context. Single-source functions and a one-sentence instruction are evaluated separately.

\subsection{Independent functional confirmation}\label{independent-functional-confirmation}

Selective formation improves mean accuracy by 2.93 {[}1.42, 4.35{]}, 3.42 {[}1.17, 5.76{]}, 7.37 {[}5.37, 9.43{]}, and 2.78 {[}0.49, 4.79{]} points in Qwen3-8B, Mistral, Llama, and Qwen3-14B. It outperforms complete cross-source formation removal in every checkpoint. Gains concentrate on conflicting correct labels; agreeing-label cases lose 2.34 {[}0.78, 4.00{]} points in Qwen3-8B and 3.61 {[}1.56, 5.86{]} in Qwen3-14B. Individual-source gains are not uniformly confirmed. Query-only isolation gives larger gains in Llama and Qwen3-14B, so selective formation is not a universally optimal policy.

Complete formation isolation can increase convention influence under full-source reading. Combining it with source-only reading gives the graph-required zero B-world effect on A. These policies use known source ownership and remove unequal edge sets; they test functional consequences. Appendix C retains all policies, source-specific scores, agreement tradeoffs, and private controls.

\subsection{Association influence and criterion-related function}\label{association-influence-and-criterion-related-function}

Source-conditioned use also depends on what a learned association does to a new input. To test this, we independently reverse B's private convention under food and service criteria, holding A fixed. Responses are projected onto food, service, bias, and their interaction. The resulting two-by-two criterion--feature matrix distinguishes changing the amount of influence from changing its functional direction.

In Qwen3-14B, native food and service selectivity are .282 {[}.199, .371{]} and .404 {[}.294, .528{]} nats. A prospective within-context normalized determinant is .410 {[}.330, .493{]}, rejecting a restricted account in which both criteria only rescale one common functional direction. With the query prohibited from reading B, it is still .190 {[}.073, .310{]}. Local label and answer-message interfaces yield different responses. Historical answer KV only partially reconstructs the \textbf{actual label-interface} criterion counterfactual (r=.668, RMSE=.646, zero RMSE=.746).

Thus association transfer, full-history criterion dependence, and correct source-conditioned use are distinct measurable properties. The complete response matrices and negative effects are retained in the appendix; a positive aspect response alone is not sufficient evidence for a complete abstract criterion program.

\section{Coordinating independently learned source rules}\label{coordinating-independently-learned-source-rules}

\subsection{Sharing identities while keeping supervision private}\label{sharing-identities-while-keeping-supervision-private}

What can be shared without exchanging private supervision? With cross-source demonstration formation blocked, we cross access to an unlabeled source-name list during formation and whole-query reading, using unrelated names as control. Qwen3-8B has a formation-specific full-output gain of 4.59 {[}2.73, 6.64{]} points, with uncertain candidate gain; Mistral's effect is negative and Llama's uncertain (Appendix G). Names alone therefore provide a partial, model-dependent account of the missing shared information.

\subsection{Sharing an output schema before private rule learning}\label{sharing-an-output-schema-before-private-rule-learning}

We next share a source-to-word-set table before independent formation, for example Alex: no, yes; Sam: pass, flag. It specifies output spaces, without review--label pairs or private polarity. An unrelated-name table has the same words and structure; a swapped table reverses the real sources' word assignments. Formation and query access are crossed while all private cross-source formation remains blocked.

True-minus-unrelated formation-only gains in full-vocabulary accuracy are 26.66 {[}23.05, 30.47{]}, 7.71 {[}5.08, 10.45{]}, and 8.30 {[}6.05, 10.84{]} points in Qwen3-8B, Mistral, and Llama. Direct reading-only contrasts include zero in all three. Public relational information therefore affects jointly usable private evidence through formation, even when no query position can read it.

Literal table routing is insufficient: correct tables outperform swapped tables, but swapped tables themselves improve full accuracy and reduce other-source outputs in every checkpoint. Qwen's candidate accuracy also falls, and some source-specific gains remain uncertain (Appendix H). The result identifies useful shared formation structure, with costs; the next test asks whether information-equivalent structures form equally compatible evidence.

\subsection{Common formation backgrounds constrain joint rule use}\label{common-formation-backgrounds-constrain-joint-rule-use}

We keep every public relation and private demonstration fixed, reversing only the two rows of the correct schema. Private cross-source formation remains blocked, and no query position can read the table. Each ordering supplies the same facts but forms its own pair of source caches. We combine A and B caches from one ordering (00 or 11), or from different orderings (01 or 10), interchanging all private K/V states at unchanged global positions.

With separate word sets, coherent composition improves full-output use much more than within-source candidate judgments (E126; Appendix I). A post-hoc diagnosis finds that most wrong-source words also match that other source's private convention. This motivates a prospective test with identical output words: a model must select the appropriate learned mapping even though the words themselves cannot identify a source.

E127 fixes four checkpoints and both shared/separate namespaces before scoring. Its primary uses shared words and opposite private conventions, where the two sources require different answers. The coherent-minus-mixed contrast averages 00 and 11 against 01 and 10. It cancels additive differences in individual-cache quality, while retaining the joint reader's nonlinear behavior.

Coherent composition improves shared-word, opposite-convention accuracy by 8.89 {[}6.64, 11.13{]}, 5.86 {[}4.10, 7.71{]}, 0.78 {[}0.10, 1.56{]}, and 9.38 {[}7.32, 11.52{]} points in Qwen3-8B, Mistral, Llama, and Qwen3-14B. The source-conditioned margin also increases in each checkpoint (Appendix J).

The same dependence therefore extends beyond selecting a distinguishable word set. The paired source-conditioned margin is one half of the gold-A-oriented difference between A and B requests. A single request-independent output shift cancels in this difference and cannot explain its positive change. This is an equivalent projection of the two-source margins, not an additional independent mechanism measure.

The caches retain strong requested-source-only functions, yet coherent joint-reading mean accuracy under conflicting conventions is only 53--65\%. The small Llama effect and nonuniform private-direction gains remain reported. With food/service criteria instead of opposing codes, coherent formation yields a smaller gain: Qwen3-14B improves by 2.93 {[}0.59, 5.47{]} points, while the other accuracy intervals touch or cross zero; joint-reading mean accuracy is about 50--53\% (Appendix K).

Local private-function accuracy therefore does not determine joint usability. Shared-word effects exceed a word-set-only explanation; request margins exclude a common output shift. Whole-cache compatibility remains consistent with contextual binding and competitive retrieval (Appendices I--K).

\section{Related work and positioning}\label{related-work-and-positioning}

Cho's circuit \citep{cho2025revisitingincontextlearninginference}, information removal \citep{cho2026mechanismtaskorientedinformationremoval}, Contextualize-then-Aggregate \citep{bakalova2025contextualizethenaggregatecircuitsincontextlearning}, and How Few-Shot Examples Add Up \citep{wang2026fewshotexamplesaddup} establish input merging, information selection, contextualization, and QK/V contributions. \citet{sun-etal-2025-interpret} trace input--label mappings, including 70B/72B checks. These explain important components of our setting. We add a measurement of whose learned relations enter evidence, an output-dependent formation prediction, and its native and functional consequences.

Local task vectors \citep{zheng-etal-2026-label} and just-in-time representations \citep{li2025justintimedistributedtaskrepresentations} establish heterogeneous task states. \citet{feng2024languagemodelsbindentities}, \citet{dai-etal-2024-representational}, and Mixing Mechanisms \citep{gurarieh2026mixingmechanismslanguagemodels} explain binding, ordering, and competing retrieval mechanisms; Test, then Route \citep{thavarasa2026testroutelanguagemodels} separates predicate testing from token-dependent routing. We examine how these frameworks apply to relations learned from multiple sources sharing output words. Parallel Context Windows \citep{ratner-etal-2023-parallel} and CacheBlend \citep{yao2025cacheblendfastlargelanguage} already address independently computed prefixes and KV context dependence. Our counterfactual fixes public facts and private positions, blocks private supervision exchange, and tests joint function under equivalent formation backgrounds.

Task recognition/learning \citep{yang2026localizingtaskrecognitiontask} separates label-space alignment from within-space mappings; \citet{li-etal-2024-language} test task composition and label remapping. Demonstration Conflict \citep{jiao2026understandingdynamicsdemonstrationconflict} studies corrupted demonstrations, and Jurisdiction \citep{li2026contextmisleadsincontextlearning} studies contextual authority over prior knowledge. Here both source rules are legitimate and must be inferred from demonstrations. \citet{yu-ananiadou-2024-large}, \citet{crosbie-shutova-2025-induction}, and Racing Thoughts \citep{lepori-etal-2025-racing} motivate operation-specific causal tests. \citet{lepori-etal-2026-language} and CoSToM \citep{li-etal-2026-costom} separate internal information from deployment; our frozen-model tests connect historical relation formation to source-conditioned function.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Source-conditioned ICL is a problem of evidence provenance as well as evidence access. We identify how shared outputs mediate the historical formation of learned associations, reconstruct naming counterfactuals, and test this operation in native judgments and larger checkpoints.

This account explains why separation policies have different consequences. Selective answer isolation can preserve private rules, while broader isolation can discard useful shared structure. Public source--output relations aid independently learned rules through formation, and equivalent formation backgrounds yield different joint usability. Which source a query reads and how its evidence was formed jointly shape source-conditioned behavior, including when its answer is correct.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The checkpoint comparisons couple model capacity with training recipe. Their results describe the computation of the tested models. Dual-aspect comments always place food before service, so the criterion-dependent responses are tied to that serialization. Cache interchanges and renormalized masks test specified interfaces while allowing other paths and subsequent state changes. Policy masks remove unequal edge sets, and reconstructions use actual donor activations.

The source-specific conventions are constructed. They let us balance legitimate conflicting rules and measure their provenance, but do not estimate how frequently such conflicts occur in deployment or their practical error cost. SST5 text may have appeared in pretraining; novelty of the private convention does not imply novelty of each review. Intervals use context resampling and are not adjusted for every comparison. Complete conditions and methodological details accompany the reported effects in the appendix.

\subsection{Materials and serialization}\label{materials-and-serialization}

Controlled experiments include single-aspect sentiment templates and dual-aspect restaurant comments. The dual-aspect task crosses food and service polarity and the source-specific criterion. Each context contains eight demonstrations per source and eight novel comments. Source names and demonstration order vary; private output directions are balanced. The model infers the source's rule from examples. Shared outputs are \texttt{no/\allowbreak{}yes}; separate outputs retain A's words and replace B's with \texttt{pass/\allowbreak{}flag}. Label words including their leading space are checked to be single tokens in the tokenizer used for that run.

Natural sentiment text comes from the Stanford Sentiment Treebank \citep{socher-etal-2013-recursive}, distributed as \texttt{SetFit/\allowbreak{}sst5}, Arrow build \texttt{e51bdcd8\allowbreak{}cd3a30da\allowbreak{}231967c1\allowbreak{}a249ba59\allowbreak{}361279a3}. We keep only original classes 0 and 4, mapped to negative and positive, and deduplicate identical text within each split. Discovery and confirmation demonstrations are drawn from opposite SHA256 text-hash parities of training. Discovery queries use official validation; confirmation queries use official test, excluding text in training and, for confirmation, validation. The command-line mode named \texttt{validation} denotes confirmation and selects the SST5 test split. Comments contain 5--64 tokens under the tested tokenizer. Selection uses gold sentiment and token geometry, with no model-output screening.

The earlier Measuring Hate Speech \citep{sachdeva-etal-2022-measuring} task uses the public \texttt{ucberkeley-dlab/\allowbreak{}measuring-hate-speech} corpus. We group by comment ID, average hate-speech and target-category annotations, keep comments of at most 50 words with mean hate-speech score at least 1, and separate race-targeted texts (race score at least .75, gender at most .1) from gender-targeted texts (gender at least .75, race at most .1). A fixed shuffle seed 5600 gives a 70/30 demonstration/query partition. Sources adopt opposite category-to-label conventions, with both private directions evaluated. These constructed rules test source-specific supervision rather than reconstructing individual annotator preferences.

For B's eight demonstrations we sample two length/offset-matched quartets, each containing two positive and two negative comments. Cross-class reassignment exchanges the positive and negative comments while retaining the label stream; within-class reassignment swaps within each polarity and moves the same number of comments. This preserves the input multiset, source names, label words, anchor positions, and all tokens outside comment content. A's eight comments and all eight queries stay fixed. Four query comments have each sentiment; the private reversal alternates across contexts. Paired-model materials are byte-identical within family and stage, while stage generators and query samples remain separate.

The input is a task continuation, with no added chat template or generated reasoning. The common header is \texttt{Below\ are\ comments\ and\ the\ labels\ that\ individual\ annotators\ gave\ them.} followed by two newlines. Each demonstration has the following literal field order, followed by two newlines:

\begin{verbatim}
Comment: <original comment>
Annotator: <source name>
Label: <one label word>
\end{verbatim}

The query uses the same fields and ends immediately after \texttt{Label:}. The block ending at that colon, the leading-space label word, and the two-newline separator are encoded separately without special tokens. This segmentation is part of the protocol. The first answer-token position is the label anchor. The historical answer region includes that word and its separator tokens. Source ownership extends across every token of its demonstration; common header tokens have no source owner.

The actual serialized token stream is part of the method. Blocks, answer words, and separators are encoded separately. For Mistral, this introduces fixed extra spaces relative to literal whole-string encoding: the first native confirmation context has 351 rather than 335 tokens. Its B answer-message region includes the answer word plus three separator tokens per demonstration, totaling 32 sender positions; Qwen uses 16. Paired token positions and boundaries remain identical across the relevant manipulations. Native controls validate the actual stream, not a claimed equivalent whole-string encoding. Source code and independent tokenizer reconstruction preserve this disclosure.

\subsection{Causal interfaces and access graphs}\label{causal-interfaces-and-access-graphs}

Formation and reading are manipulated separately. In a formation-message interchange, donor K, V, or KV at historical B-answer positions replace the corresponding inputs to attention only for A-label receivers at each layer. The implementation calls the model's native eager attention with the full original matrix geometry, retaining recipient queries and the causal mask; only selected receiver outputs are substituted. Other receiver outputs remain native, and subsequent states evolve normally. Donor keys are taken from the actual layer cache, with its positional transformation already applied. Cached-state interchanges instead replace selected A/B K/V positions in a deep copy of a completed prefix; they leave global positions and the other recipient cache positions unchanged.

Native-operation tests have no donor. They set the specified historical attention edges to negative infinity before softmax. Targeted removal blocks earlier B-answer regions at A-label receivers; within-source removal blocks A-answer regions there. Complete B-to-A removal excludes all earlier B-owned positions from every A-owned receiver, and restricted restoration reopens only earlier B-answer-to-A-label edges in that graph. Symmetric functional policies apply the source-swapped rule to both groups in one prefix. Masks renormalize attention, remove unequal edge sets, and permit downstream state changes.

Reading restrictions apply to every live query token, including source-name and comment tokens, across every layer. They exclude the unrequested source's complete prefix positions while retaining the common background, requested-source positions and causal query history. A-only reading therefore restricts direct access; it retains any earlier B influence already formed in the allowed evidence. The eight queries are batched with left padding and explicit positions starting after the prefix. Each scoring call uses a fresh deep copy of the pristine cache; padded tokens are masked and cache sequence length and storage independence are checked.

Public-schema experiments block private cross-source formation, independently crossing access to the public source--word-set table during prefix formation and whole-query reading. The table has no comment--label pairs or private polarity. Equivalent-background experiments reverse its two rows, then cross A/B private caches from the two backgrounds at identical positions. Coherent compositions use 00/11 and mixed compositions use 01/10. The query cannot read the public table; these tests concern the joint usability of independently formed private evidence.

\subsection{Scores, aggregation and uncertainty}\label{scores-aggregation-and-uncertainty}

Let \(z_{cq}\) be the second-minus-first candidate-label logit difference and \(g_{cq}\in\{-1,+1\}\) the requested source's gold direction. For a B-rule intervention, the context response is \(D_c=Q^{-1}\sum_q g_{cq}(z^{cross}_{cq}-z^{origin}_{cq})\). Signed effects compare these context responses; absolute effects first take \(|D_c|\), then form the paired contrast. Likewise, the fixed-word relational excess compares the magnitudes of context-averaged cross-class and within-class effects, rather than averaging absolute per-query differences.

Candidate accuracy restricts the decision to the requested source's two words. Full-output accuracy requires the highest-logit token in the entire vocabulary to equal the correct word. Joint correctness requires both source requests to be correct for the same input/world. Mean accuracy over sources is a different measure. Real-text function-policy primaries (E123/E131) give equal weight to origin/cross worlds, both sources and all queries within each context; the within-class world remains a separate control. Controlled criterion--convention policy tests (E122) average all four B criterion/convention worlds. Single-source and instruction controls are evaluated separately. Gold-agreement strata are determined by the two requested-source answers, with no filtering by observed correctness.

Formation reconstruction compares the actual namespace-donor interface effect with the historical message-interchange effect using coefficient one. Raw predictions retain both rule worlds; rule-double contrasts subtract the origin effect from the cross effect. Pearson correlation and RMSE summarize those predefined targets, with the zero-prediction RMSE as reference. No slope, offset, channel weight or intervention strength is fitted.

We use 10,000 percentile bootstrap resamples of whole contexts. Each draw preserves all queries, worlds, source requests and paired model conditions belonging to that context. Context-level contrast seeds are 910; reconstruction uses 109910. Correlations are computed from flattened targets with whole-context resampling, not an average of context-wise correlations. Main comparisons use fresh confirmation n32 after discovery n16. The original native/formation/function discovery seeds are 120001/121001/123001 and confirmation seeds are 120101/121101/123101. E131 retains the discovery seeds and uses new confirmation seeds 131101/131102/131103. These are finite checkpoint comparisons, with training recipes allowed to differ across sizes.

\section{Complete criterion--convention functional response}\label{complete-criterionconvention-functional-response}

\subsection{Separating criterion from private output convention}\label{separating-criterion-from-private-output-convention}

A learned association can change a classifier's confidence without specifying a complete criterion for a new input. We test this distinction with comments containing independently balanced food and service sentiment. Source A's demonstrations establish a food criterion. Source B can evaluate either food or service, under an output convention agreeing with or opposite to A's. The criteria are inferred from demonstrations rather than stated in the multi-source prompt (E117/E118).

The four B worlds are constructed by bijectively reassigning inputs to fixed demonstration positions. A's raw demonstrations, B's output stream, source names, positions, and input multiset remain unchanged. Eight novel queries cover all four sentiment combinations. Every interface is evaluated on the same queries: complete native histories, histories whose queries cannot read B positions, A-label cache interchange into one recipient, and historical B-answer messages consumed during A-label formation. Separate-output label donors test the effect of output coding during formation while restoring the shared-output recipient for querying.

Let \(f_q,s_q\in\{-1,+1\}\) be food and service polarity, oriented to A's private convention. For criterion \(t\), define \(\delta_t(q)=\frac{1}{2}[z_{t,+}(q)-z_{t,-}(q)]\), the response to changing B's convention. The context-level matrix

\[
M_c=\begin{pmatrix}
\mathbb E_q[\delta_{food}(q)f_q] & \mathbb E_q[\delta_{food}(q)s_q]\\
\mathbb E_q[\delta_{service}(q)f_q] & \mathbb E_q[\delta_{service}(q)s_q]
\end{pmatrix}
\]

separates the two criteria's effects on the two aspects. Bias and food--service interaction are also retained. These are B-relation effects while A is requested, not B's own accuracy or a gain in correctly isolating A.

\subsection{Association magnitude and criterion selectivity give different answers}\label{association-magnitude-and-criterion-selectivity-give-different-answers}

Across three model families, E117 confirmation finds that changing B's pairing alters both food and service sensitivity through A-label caches. A positive service response alone therefore cannot establish transport of B's service criterion. E118 independently crosses criterion with B's convention and preserves the entire response matrix. Full native histories show more criterion selectivity than local label interchanges in several settings, with substantial family variation. The mean criterion--feature interaction is positive in all three families, but Qwen3-8B's interaction mainly reflects service selectivity; unequal gains along one common direction can also produce a positive mean interaction.
